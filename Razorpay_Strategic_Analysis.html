01 · Evidence Base

Data Integrity & Corrections

Before any framework is applied, the underlying numbers have to survive inspection. Four defects in the source data materially change the analysis. Each is documented here with the CMIE evidence that establishes it.

“Operating Margin = Operating Income ÷ Revenue. Take 3-year average for each company. Verify source; don't use AI blindly.”S1-9 deck, Step 3

Correction 1 · The margin formula

CMIE's Income & Expenditure Summary reports a single Total expenses figure. Expanding it shows why it cannot be used as an operating cost base:

CMIE line itemBelongs in operating cost?Why
Operating expensesYesCost of processing, bank/network fees, employee cost, S&D, power
Financial services expensesYesCost of the lending/financial-services revenue booked above the line
Non-cash charges (depreciation & amortisation)YesRecurring capital consumption
Provision for direct taxNoBelow the operating line by definition
Prior period & extraordinary expensesNoNon-recurring; the deck's definition is an operating margin

The formula used throughout this report is therefore:

Operating revenue = Total income − Prior-period & extraordinary income
Operating income (EBIT) = Operating revenue − Operating expenses − Financial-services expenses − Non-cash charges
Operating margin = Operating income ÷ Operating revenue

Correction 2 · Razorpay Software Ltd. is not the operating business in FY24–FY25

The single largest error in the previous draft was treating Razorpay Software Ltd.'s FY24 and FY25 filings as the payment business. Four independent CMIE fields show the entity changed character:

A · Classification flip

CMIE Main product/service moves from Commission agents services (FY18–FY23) to Securities investment services (FY24, FY25, Latest). Industry group moves from Other miscellaneous services to Other fund based financial services.

B · Product-line disappearance

“Trade & commissioning agents' services” sales value is ₹2,267.72 Cr in FY23 — 99.4% of total income. In FY24 and FY25 the line is blank, replaced only by communication services (₹25.89 / ₹51.62 Cr), rent (₹2.78 / ₹2.99 Cr) and interest.

C · P&L collapse and substitution

Income from non-financial services falls from ₹2,270.10 Cr (FY23) to ₹29.01 Cr (FY24). Simultaneously Prior period & extraordinary income jumps to ₹2,058.51 Cr (FY24) and ₹2,206.14 Cr (FY25).

D · The arithmetic sanity check

Employee cost as a share of reported operating revenue is 143.8% in FY24 and 115.4% in FY25. No live operating business pays its staff more than its revenue; the denominator is wrong, not the company.

Conclusion. The last clean operating year for Razorpay in CMIE is FY23: operating revenue ₹2,279.76 Cr, EBIT ₹12.42 Cr, margin +0.54%. FY24–FY25 are reported as n.m. throughout this report, with a reconstructed core-revenue figure used only where a denominator is unavoidable (market share, Step 3 weighting).

Correction 3 · PayU's FY25 margin was an impairment, not economics

PayU reports a FY25 PAT of −₹3,567.80 Cr. Of this, ₹3,536.23 Cr sits in prior-period & extraordinary expenses — an impairment, not a trading loss. On an operating basis PayU's FY25 margin is −1.05%, an improvement on FY24's −4.99%.

A second PayU caveat. From FY24, CMIE reclassifies PayU's revenue from Income from non-financial services to Income from financial services (₹3,369.79 Cr in FY24, ₹3,939.66 Cr in FY25), with a matching move of cost into Financial services expenses (₹2,955.94 Cr / ₹3,397.99 Cr). PayU's later years therefore blend payment processing with its consumer-credit book. Its margin is not a pure payments margin; treat the comparison with care.

03 · Step 1

Define the Industry

An industry definition is a decision, not a description. Draw the boundary too wide and every force looks weak; draw it too narrow and the analysis describes a company rather than an industry.

“Be specific. The industry should be bounded by product/service, geography and customer type. Don't confuse the industry with the market.”S1-9 deck, Step 1

The definition used in this report

Third-party online payment aggregation and gateway services for digital-first merchants in India.
Product/service — RBI-licensed payment aggregation and gateway processing: collecting an online payment from a payer, routing it across card, UPI, netbanking and wallet rails, settling it to a merchant, and the reconciliation, refund, risk and reporting layer wrapped around that flow. Revenue is a take-rate on transaction value.
Geography — India. The RBI's Payment Aggregator licence is a national, jurisdiction-specific licence; data localisation rules mean the operating asset cannot be shared across borders.
Customer type — Digital-first businesses that collect online — e-commerce, SaaS, D2C, edtech, gaming, online travel, and the digital collection arms of BFSI and utilities. From single-founder sign-ups to large enterprises.

The adjacency exclusion is the analytically important one. Razorpay's RazorpayX (neobanking), Capital (lending) and Payroll sit outside this industry definition. That is correct for Step 1 — they serve different needs with different value chains. But it means the industry we are analysing is precisely the part of Razorpay's business where the economics are worst, and the escape routes lie outside the boundary. Step 1 defines the arena; it does not oblige the firm to stay in it.

Industry vs market — keeping the two apart

The industry (supply side)

The set of firms that produce payment-aggregation services and compete for the same merchants using substitutable capabilities: Razorpay, PayU, CCAvenue, BillDesk, Cashfree, Easebuzz, Paytm PG, PhonePe PG, plus the direct-integration desks of the large acquiring banks.

The market (demand side)

Indian online merchants needing to collect digital payments — sized by transaction value, merchant count and take-rate. The market can grow 25% a year while the industry earns nothing, and in this case it does. Market growth and industry profitability are independent variables.

Market structure — which model actually fits

The deck distinguishes perfect competition, monopoly and oligopoly (Bertrand, collusive, Cournot). Getting this right determines what Five Forces will conclude.

StructurePredictionFit with the evidence
Perfect competitionPrice = marginal cost; zero economic profit; atomistic sellersPartial. The zero-profit result holds (+3.1% weighted margin) but sellers are not atomistic — top three hold 76.7%
MonopolyOne seller, price above cost, sustained profitNo. Five credible national players plus bank desks
Cournot oligopolyFirms compete on quantity; price falls with total output but stays above cost; positive profits persistNo. Positive profits do not persist — four-peer EBIT fell from ₹477 Cr to −₹82 Cr in three years
Bertrand oligopolyFirms compete on price; with near-identical products price is competed down to cost even with very few sellersYes. Few sellers, high concentration, near-zero industry margin, price as the primary competitive weapon
Collusive oligopolyFew firms sustain supra-competitive pricesNo. Ruled out by a national MDR framework, a regulator watching pricing, and a VC-funded entrant with no profit constraint
Step 1's central finding. This is a Bertrand oligopoly: concentrated in share, competitive in price. The 76.7% top-three concentration figure would normally signal pricing power; here it signals nothing, because with undifferentiated output and low switching costs, three players are enough to compete price down to cost. High concentration and zero profit are not a contradiction — in Bertrand competition they are the expected result. Every subsequent finding in this report follows from that.

Why entry barriers are moderate-to-low

Cloud infrastructure removes capital intensity. Bank and network rails are available to any licensee on similar terms. The RBI PA licence is a real gate — but it is an administrative gate, passable with capital and patience, and the regulator has issued it to dozens of firms including Cashfree, Easebuzz and Juspay. A licence that many hold is not a moat.

Why exit barriers are high

Merchant settlement obligations, escrow and nodal-account balances, regulatory wind-down requirements, and multi-year enterprise contracts mean a sub-scale player cannot simply stop. Cashfree's four consecutive loss years at flat revenue is what a high exit barrier looks like from the inside: continuing to fund a business that cannot reach profitability because stopping is costlier than persisting.

Implication. Low entry plus high exit is the worst quadrant for incumbent profitability: new capacity keeps arriving and failing capacity does not leave. The five-year margin path in this report — positive, then eroding, then negative — is the signature of that quadrant.

Why the weighted average is the right statistic — and where it hides something

What weighting captures

It answers “what does a rupee of revenue in this industry earn?” — the question a capital allocator asks. Cashfree's −17% margin should not count as much as BillDesk's +12% when Cashfree is a twentieth of the revenue.

What weighting conceals

A +3.13% average across a spread of −17.2% to +11.9% is a statement about the middle of a bimodal distribution, not about a typical firm. No company in this industry earns +3.13%. 06 · Step 4

Industry Structure · The Five Forces

Step 3 established that this industry earns +3.13% heading to zero. Step 4 has to explain why — and a correct analysis must produce a net-unattractive verdict, because the margins already told us it is one. Each force below is rated, evidenced, and traced through to its effect on industry prices and industry costs.

“Analyse the five forces for the industry you defined. For each force, rate it and justify with evidence. Tie the forces back to the industry's prices and costs — that is the only reason the framework matters.”S1-9 deck, Step 4
1 · Bargaining Power of Suppliers
Very high — the binding constraint
Who
Issuing and acquiring banks, card networks (Visa, Mastercard, RuPay), NPCI for UPI, and cloud infrastructure providers.
Evidence
Operating expenses consume 89–98% of operating revenue at every player: Infibeam 97.7% (FY26), BillDesk 86.4% (FY24), Razorpay 97.7% (FY23), PayU 93.5% (FY22). The dominant component is interchange, scheme fees and bank charges — payable at rates the aggregator does not set.
Why it binds
The MDR pool is fixed by the networks and the regulator. Aggregators bargain only over the thin residual slice, and the largest single input — interchange — is administered, not negotiated. No aggregator has the volume to change a scheme fee.
Price effect
Caps industry price. The merchant pays an all-in MDR; the aggregator's share is whatever survives after the supplier takes its administered cut.
Cost effect
Sets a floor under industry cost that no amount of operating efficiency can breach. This is why even the most ruthless cost operator in the sample lands at +1.57%.
Verdict
The single most important force. It explains why the industry's profit pool is structurally thin regardless of how well any individual firm is run.
2 · Bargaining Power of Buyers
High — and rising
Who
Merchants. Two distinct segments: enterprise/institutional accounts and the long tail of SMB and startup merchants.
Evidence
BillDesk — the enterprise-facing player — lost 11.2 points of share and 18.3% of revenue in FY24 while carrying the industry's best margin. Infibeam gained 11.5 points on a cost structure with employee cost at 0.8% of revenue. Buyers moved to the cheaper supplier and the market rewarded them for it.
Why it binds
Switching cost is genuinely low. Integration is a few days of developer work; most large merchants run two or three gateways in parallel for redundancy and routing arbitrage, which removes the lock-in that a single integration might otherwise create. Multi-homing is standard practice, not an exception.
Price effect
Pushes industry price down continuously. Enterprise merchants extract basis points at every renewal; the existence of a live alternative makes the threat credible every time.
Cost effect
Raises industry cost via customer-acquisition and retention spend — the sales, support and integration-engineering burden of winning accounts that can leave cheaply.
Asymmetry worth noting
The long tail is less price-sensitive and more service-sensitive — a solo founder values a one-hour onboarding over 5 basis points. This is the only segment where buyer power is weaker, and it is the segment Razorpay is strongest in. It is the foundation of the recommendation on page 15.
3 · Rivalry Among Existing Competitors
Very high — price-based and destructive
Who
Five named players plus Paytm PG, PhonePe PG, Easebuzz, Juspay and the bank direct-integration desks.
Evidence
Four-peer operating income fell ₹477 Cr → ₹291 Cr → ₹111 Cr → −₹82 Cr (FY22–FY25) while revenue grew every year. Infibeam's margin compressed from +9.73% to +1.57% while its revenue grew 4.1×. Growth is being bought, not earned.
Why it binds
Four structural amplifiers, all present at once. (a) Near-identical output — a successful transaction is a successful transaction; there is no premium version of “the payment went through.” (b) High fixed, near-zero marginal cost — every incremental transaction is worth taking at almost any price, which is the classic setup for price war. (c) Funded rivals with no profit constraint — Cashfree ran four straight loss years at flat revenue. (d) High exit barriers — settlement and regulatory obligations mean losers stay in the game.
Price effect
Drives industry price toward cost. This is the Bertrand mechanism from Step 1 operating in the data.
Cost effect
Raises industry cost through duplicated engineering, competitive S&D, and the reliability arms race — every player must fund 99.99% uptime because a rival advertises it.
Verdict
Rivalry and supplier power together account for essentially all of the industry's margin problem. The remaining two forces are secondary.
4 · Threat of New Entrants
Moderate-to-high
Who
New PA licensees, adjacent platforms integrating payments (Shopify, Zoho, ONDC participants), and banks moving forward into aggregation.
Evidence
The RBI has issued Payment Aggregator authorisation to dozens of firms. Cashfree reached ₹640 Cr of revenue from a standing start; Easebuzz and Juspay are live at scale. A barrier that dozens of firms have crossed is not a barrier.
What raises the barrier
RBI licensing and compliance (PCI-DSS, data localisation, escrow/nodal account requirements), net-worth thresholds, bank sponsorship, and the fraud-and-risk capability that only accumulates with transaction history.
What lowers it
Cloud removes capital intensity. Rails are available to all licensees on broadly similar terms. Orchestration layers such as Juspay let a merchant integrate once and route anywhere — which lets an entrant reach merchants without winning the integration.
Price effect
Caps industry price — any visible margin invites entry, so the incumbent cannot price to it.
Cost effect
Raises industry cost via a rising compliance floor that every player, new or old, must fund.
Nuance
Regulation is doing two contradictory things at once: raising the cost of entry and standardising the product so that entry, once made, is immediately competitive. On net it has not protected incumbent margin — the data says so.
5 · Threat of Substitutes
Moderate
Who
Direct bank integration, merchant-side in-house payment stacks, UPI collect links and static QR, payment-orchestration layers, and account-to-account rails.
Evidence
UPI's zero-MDR regime removes the revenue pool for an entire and growing share of transactions. Large merchants such as major airlines and marketplaces run direct bank integrations for their highest-volume flows and use aggregators only for the residual mix.
Why it is not higher
Substitutes handle the transaction but not the system. Multi-rail routing, retries, reconciliation, refunds, dispute handling, settlement reporting and fraud scoring are real work that a UPI link does not do. For any merchant with meaningful volume and a finance function, the aggregator is still the cheaper option in total cost.
Price effect
Pushes industry price down — the availability of a zero-MDR rail anchors merchant expectations about what payments should cost.
Cost effect
Broadly neutral on industry cost.
Direction of travel
Rising. Each new zero-cost rail and each improvement in orchestration software shrinks the pool the industry can charge against.

The forces are not fixed — where a firm can act on structure

Weaken supplier power

Build direct bank relationships at volume; shift transaction mix toward rails with lower administered cost; win the routing decision so the aggregator, not the bank, chooses where a transaction goes. Owning the routing logic is the closest thing in this industry to owning a supply chain.

Weaken buyer power

Raise switching cost by moving from a single integration to an embedded system of record — payouts, reconciliation, vendor payments, payroll and a current account. A merchant can re-point a payment API in a week; it cannot re-point its treasury in a week. This is the only force Razorpay has genuine leverage over.

Soften rivalry

Stop competing where price is the only variable. Choose segments and needs where rivals are structurally unwilling to follow — which requires real trade-offs, not just a different sales pitch. See page 09.

Live with the rest

Substitutes and the entry regime are largely regulatory and cannot be moved by a single firm. The strategic response is not to fight them but to sit where they do less damage — in the workflow layer above the rail, where a zero-MDR rail is an input rather than a competitor.

07 · The Bridge

From Industry Structure to Relative Position

Industry structure explains the average. It cannot explain a 29-point spread between the best and worst performer inside the same structure. That requires a second, different question.

Why are some firms more profitable than others?

Industry structure — the average

Answered by the five forces. Determines the average industry price and average industry cost, and therefore the size of the profit pool available to anyone. Pages 03–06

Verdict for this industry: a pool of roughly +3% of revenue, shrinking.

Relative position — the spread

Answered by the value chain: differences in activities produce a relative firm price and a relative firm cost that differ from the industry average. Pages 08–11

Verdict for Razorpay: it sits essentially at the industry average on both price and cost — which is the diagnosis.

The identity that connects them

Competitive advantage = (Relative firm price − Average industry price) − (Relative firm cost − Average industry cost)

→ Positive because of the first bracket = differentiation: the firm commands a price premium.
→ Positive because of the second bracket = cost leadership: the firm operates below industry cost.
→ Both brackets near zero = no competitive advantage, and the firm earns the industry average — which here is approximately nothing.

The generic strategies, and the two distinctions that are usually missed

Cost leadership

Become the lowest-cost producer at acceptable quality, then choose whether to keep the gap as margin or spend it on share. Claimed by: Infibeam/CCAvenue. Evidence: employee cost at 0.8% of revenue, 4.1× revenue growth, margin surrendered to +1.57% — it spent the gap.

Differentiation

Offer something buyers will pay more for, at a cost increment smaller than the premium. Claimed by: Razorpay. Evidence: a cost increment of 20+ points of employee cost, and a price premium of approximately zero. The claim fails on the second half.

Focus

Serve a narrow segment better than a broad player can, accepting the rest of the market as lost. Claimed by: BillDesk. Evidence: the industry's best margin for three years — and then share loss, because it defended the segment and not the position.

Cost leader ≠ cost-effective

Every firm in this industry is trying to be cost-effective. Only one is the cost leader — a position, held by a single firm, that others cannot occupy simultaneously. Razorpay's cost-reduction programmes make it more cost-effective; they do not make it the cost leader, because Infibeam is 20× leaner on labour. Pursuing cost leadership from third place is the most expensive way to arrive at parity.

Different ≠ differentiated

Razorpay is genuinely different: better documentation, faster onboarding, a broader product surface. It is not differentiated, because difference only counts when buyers pay for it. The FY22–23 margins of +0.55% and +0.54% are the market's verdict: the difference is real, visible, appreciated — and free.

Stuck in the middle. Razorpay carries a differentiator's cost structure (employee cost at 25–28% of revenue, the second-highest in the sample) and earns a cost-parity price (+0.5% margins, the same as the industry average). That is the textbook definition. It is not a failure of execution — Razorpay executes well — it is a failure to choose. The two firms that made a choice, BillDesk and Infibeam, are the only two that earned a return.

Holding both a cost and a differentiation advantage is possible but rare, and it needs a structural reason — a technology discontinuity, a network effect, or an activity system rivals cannot copy piecemeal.

Keep and invest — price drivers with switching cost

Keep but stop subsidising — strong, unpriced

09 · Relative Position

Trade-offs, Fit & the Five Tests

A distinctive value chain is not enough. It has to embed choices rivals are unwilling to copy, the activities have to reinforce one another, and the whole thing has to survive the five tests of a real strategy.

Trade-offs — what Razorpay must decide not to do

A trade-off exists when two positions are genuinely incompatible. There are three ways that happens.

1 · Inconsistent image or reputation

You cannot be the trusted institutional processor for a nationalised bank's collections and the fastest sign-up for an unincorporated D2C brand. The procurement standards contradict each other. BillDesk chose the first; Razorpay is trying to hold both.

2 · Activities themselves differ

Self-serve onboarding requires automated risk scoring and no human in the loop. Enterprise onboarding requires bespoke underwriting, legal negotiation and a named implementation team. The same organisation cannot optimise both; attempting it produces Razorpay's 25–28% employee cost.

3 · Limits on internal coordination and control

A firm running SMB self-serve, enterprise sales, a lending book, a neobank and payroll has five different operating cadences, risk appetites and P&L logics. Coordination cost is a real cost, and it shows up in exactly the line where Razorpay is an outlier.

Razorpay has made no trade-off. It serves startups and enterprises, self-serve and sales-led, payments and banking and lending and payroll, price-led and value-led merchants. Each addition was individually defensible. Together they produce a chain optimised for nothing, which is the mechanism behind the +0.5% margin. The absence of a trade-off is not neutrality — it is a choice to be average, paid for at a premium cost structure.

Resource-Based View · Is the Advantage Sustainable?

Position explains why a firm is ahead today. It does not explain why it stays ahead. A resource has to clear four gates in sequence, and failing any one of them determines the outcome — including the last gate, which is the one most analyses skip.

Each resource is tested in order. A “no” at any gate ends the test and fixes the verdict.

Scale, scope and learning — what changes when the operating model is software

A traditional firm's returns to scale diminish: the second bakery is harder to run than the first. A firm whose core operating processes run on data and algorithms faces a different curve — scale, scope and learning reinforce each other instead of trading off.

Scale

Weak on the rail. Infibeam's 4.1× revenue growth produced margin compression, not expansion — because per-transaction supplier cost does not fall with volume.

Real above it. Reconciliation logic, underwriting models and payroll compliance engines are built once and serve every additional merchant at near-zero marginal cost. This is where scale economies actually live in this business.

Scope

Currently a cost, not a benefit. Five products with five onboarding flows, five support desks and five risk processes is scope without economies — visible directly in the 25–28% employee-cost ratio.

Becomes a benefit once unified. One merchant record serving collections, payouts, credit and payroll means each new product costs less than the last. This is page 09's third-order fit, expressed as an operating model.

Learning

The one compounding asset. Every transaction improves fraud detection, success-rate routing and credit underwriting. Unlike brand or product, a rival cannot buy the accumulated history.

Currently siloed. Payments data, payout data and payroll data are not fused into one model per merchant. Fusing them is the single highest-leverage technical project available.

Why this matters for the recommendation. An AI-and-data operating model does not diminish with scale — but only in the activities where the marginal cost of serving the next merchant is genuinely near zero. On the rail, the marginal cost is an interchange fee and nothing compounds. In the workflow layer, the marginal cost is compute and everything compounds. Razorpay's cost problem and its learning opportunity have the same solution: move the centre of gravity of the business to where software economics apply.

It is an enabler of the strategy on page 15, not a substitute for it.
Platform and network-effect assessments distinguish throughout between the payment rail and the workflow layer above it, because the two have materially different economics and conflating them is the error that produces overstated platform claims in this industry.

15 · Recommendation

One Recommendation, With Projections

Every strand of the analysis converges on the same problem: Razorpay holds rare, hard-to-imitate assets and monetises them through an activity whose price is going to zero. One recommendation follows.

Recommendation. Reposition Razorpay from a payment gateway that sells extra products into the financial-operations system that digital-first Indian businesses run their money on — and make the three decisions that repositioning requires:

1 · Price the system, not the transaction. Stop bundling reconciliation, payouts, vendor management and payroll free behind an MDR. Charge a platform fee per merchant for the workflow layer and treat processing as a competitively-priced utility inside it.
2 · Unify one operating layer beneath all five products. One onboarding, one KYC, one risk engine, one support system, one merchant record — instead of five of each. This is where the labour-intensity gap closes without cutting capability.
3 · Refuse price-led enterprise volume. Decline RFPs won on basis points. Accept a smaller share of a larger market. This is the trade-off that makes the other two credible.

The Questions a Client Will Ask

1 · Define the industry. Who are the main players? What is average industry profitability? What drives it? How do you see it changing?

The industry. Third-party online payment aggregation and gateway services for digital-first merchants in India — collecting an online payment, routing it across card, UPI, netbanking and wallet rails, settling it to the merchant, and the reconciliation and risk layer around it. It excludes banks and card networks, which are suppliers; consumer wallets, which serve payers not merchants; and offline card acceptance, which is a different business.

The players. Five entities cover the industry: PayU (₹3,417 Cr FY24 revenue, 30.9% of the sample), Infibeam/CCAvenue (₹2,980 Cr, 26.9%), Razorpay (₹2,088 Cr, 18.9%), BillDesk (₹1,938 Cr, 17.5%) and Cashfree (₹640 Cr, 5.8%). The top three hold 76.7%.

Average profitability. +3.1% operating margin, revenue-weighted, across FY22–FY24 — ₹899 Cr of operating income on ₹28,764 Cr of revenue. The number is fragile in one direction only: single-year FY24 is +1.0%, and the four-peer total for FY25 is negative.

What drives it — two things, and only two. First, banks and card networks take 70% of every rupee of revenue at rates the aggregators do not set. Second, the product is close to identical across providers and merchants switch cheaply, so the remaining 30% is competed away on price. Concentration is high and it buys nothing: three players are enough to compete price down to cost when the output is undifferentiated.

How it changes, over a three-to-five-year horizon. Volume grows sharply with digitisation; take-rates fall toward zero as UPI's zero-fee regime expands. Absolute industry operating income has already gone ₹477 Cr → ₹291 Cr → ₹111 Cr → −₹82 Cr across FY22–FY25 while revenue grew every single year. The way this industry makes money is being removed, while what makes it valuable — the merchant relationships, the integrations, the data — is not. The survivors will be the firms that earn a majority of revenue from something other than a processing fee.

2 · How is Razorpay positioned versus competitors, and what is its current strategy?

Position: the middle, at a premium cost. Razorpay spends 25–28% of revenue on people. BillDesk spends 4–7%; Infibeam/CCAvenue spends 0.8%; Cashfree spends 38%. Razorpay charges roughly the industry price. A premium cost base earning an average price produces an average margin, and it does: +0.55% in FY22 and +0.54% in FY23, the last two years its operating business can be cleanly observed.

Current strategy: no choice has been made. Razorpay serves startups and enterprises, self-serve and sales-led, price-led and value-led merchants, across payments, banking, lending and payroll. Each addition was individually sensible; together they produce a business optimised for nothing. Its best capabilities — documentation, onboarding in hours, broad support, a full product suite — are real, visibly better than rivals', and given away free. Being different is not the same as being paid for being different.

The contrast that makes the point. The two firms that made a choice are the only two that earned a return: BillDesk chose a narrow institutional segment (+11.9% over three years) and Infibeam chose to be the cheapest (+7.8%). The two that did not choose — PayU and Cashfree — lost money. Razorpay sits at break-even between them.

3 · What are Razorpay's sources of sustainable advantage? Does it leverage them?

It has genuinely rare assets. It captures almost none of their value.

Three things Razorpay owns that rivals cannot readily obtain: a complete merchant money system — collections, payouts, a current account, credit and payroll on one merchant record, which no sampled competitor offers; a position as the default choice for new Indian digital businesses, built in 2015–2020 in conditions that no longer exist and cannot be bought back; and an engineering culture whose output rivals can see but whose source they cannot identify well enough to copy.

Recreating the first would require a payments licence, a banking partner, a lending arm, a payroll product and years of merchant transaction history, assembled and integrated. That is capital plus time plus regulatory approval plus organisational learning. It is a real barrier and it holds for roughly two to three years.

And it is not being leveraged. The products are bundled free rather than priced as a system. Onboarding, identity verification, risk and support are duplicated five times rather than shared once — which is most of why the people cost is three to six times the profitable peers'. The sales conversation still leads with the processing rate. The firm is organised to sell payments with extras attached, not to sell the system.

So the honest answer is: Razorpay has the raw material for a durable advantage and does not currently have one. The constraint is not competition and not capability. It is that the company is not arranged around its own best asset — and that is a decision, not a deficiency.

4 · Is the current strategy appropriate going forward? One recommendation.

No. The current strategy asks Razorpay to win a price contest against a rival that spends 0.8% of revenue on people, in an industry where the price is heading to zero by regulation. It cannot be won and it is not worth winning.

Recommendation: become the financial-operations system that digital-first Indian businesses run their money on, and price it as one.

Three decisions. Charge for the system — a platform fee per merchant for reconciliation, payouts, vendor management and payroll, with processing priced competitively inside it rather than carrying everything. Build one operating layer — a single onboarding, verification, risk and support system beneath all five products instead of five of each. Refuse volume won on price — decline enterprise deals decided on basis points and accept a smaller share of a larger market.

Projected outcome: operating margin from roughly zero today to +3.8% (FY26), +7.4% (FY27), +11.6% (FY28), +15.9% (FY29) — approximately ₹580 Cr of operating income on ₹3,650 Cr of revenue, with a third of revenue coming from software-like sources that a zero-fee payment rail cannot touch.

Against doing nothing: roughly ₹4,380 Cr of revenue at −3.0% margin — ₹730 Cr more revenue and ₹711 Cr less profit. That is not a forecast; it is what already happened to the four peers between FY22 and FY25.

What has to be true for this to work, and how to test it cheaply. Merchants must pay for workflow they currently receive free. Price the new tier for new merchants only, in three merchant clusters, for two quarters. If price realisation above the processing rate is negligible, the thesis is wrong and the alternative is the opposite strategy — cut costs to the bone and compete as the cheapest processor. Two quarters and a price list is what it costs to find out.

The uncomfortable part. Market share will fall. That is not a side effect to be managed — it is the evidence that the strategy is being executed. Any version of this plan that also promises share growth has not made the trade-off, and will produce the same +0.5% margin as today.

Limits — what this analysis cannot tell you

Revenue is not volume

CMIE reports statutory revenue, not total payment volume, merchant count or take-rate. Every share figure here is a revenue-concentration share within the five sampled entities. It is a reasonable proxy for economic weight and a poor proxy for transaction volume.

Gross versus net recognition

Infibeam/CCAvenue books ₹7,445 Cr of operating expense against ₹7,623 Cr of FY26 revenue — a gross-up of interchange and scheme fees. BillDesk's ratio looks structurally different. Margin levels are therefore imperfectly comparable across firms. Margin trends within a firm, absolute operating income across firms, and the employee-cost ratio are all reliable.

Standalone, not consolidated

These are single-entity filings. Group structures, subsidiaries and offshore entities are not captured. PayU's FY24–25 figures visibly blend payments with its consumer-credit book following a CMIE reclassification, which is flagged wherever those years are used.

Incomplete coverage

BillDesk ends at Mar 2024. Cashfree is missing Mar 2021. Only Infibeam/CCAvenue reports Mar 2026. The FY25 and FY26 industry pictures are partial and are not used for the headline Step 3 figure.

Sample excludes two large players

Paytm PG and PhonePe PG are material in the market and have no separable gateway P&L. Including them would lower measured concentration and, on their reported unit economics, would not raise the industry margin — so the conclusions are robust to their omission, but the share figures are specific to the sample.

Projections are a model, not a forecast

The FY26–FY29 numbers on page 15 are built from the observed FY23 cost structure with every assumption stated inline. They are intended to size the opportunity and to be varied, not to be relied on as a prediction.
